Challenge idea library

112 ideas for your next 30-day challenge

Every one of them is a daily challenge: clearly checkable in the evening, doable on every single day, sustainable for one month. Pick one, and the app reminds you to stick with it.

Sports Health Learning Creativity Focus Finance Everyday Other

How to spot a good monthly challenge: it’s just done or not done, it’s possible on every single day, and it rarely takes more than 30 minutes. All 112 examples on this list qualify.

Sports

Sports challenges: get moving

Fitness challenges are the most common way in and the most rewarding for the 30-day format, because the body responds visibly fast. The only thing that matters is setting the bar low enough for your busiest days: better 30 days small than five days ambitious.

15 ideas

Sports
“Did 30 minutes of exercise”

The type doesn’t matter, the repetition does. After 30 days, exercise is no longer an appointment, it’s the default.

Sports
“Walked 10,000 steps”

Needs zero equipment, and the gaps in your day fill themselves: after a month you take the longer route without thinking.

Sports
“Did 20 push-ups”

Two minutes of effort with measurable progress: what wears you out on day 1 is your warm-up by day 30.

Sports
“Stretched for 15 minutes”

Few things respond to daily repetition like flexibility does. One month is enough to feel the difference.

Sports
“Cycled instead of driving”

Attaches exercise to a trip you make anyway, so the challenge needs no time of its own.

Sports
“Took the stairs, not the elevator”

The perfect starter challenge: so small it’s hard to fail, yet still a real check mark every day.

Sports
“5 minutes of plank & core”

Short enough for any evening, and progression happens on its own: just hold a few seconds longer each week.

Sports
“Jogged around the block”

Only demands the start, not the distance. The route grows all by itself over 30 days.

Sports
“Walked around the block after lunch”

Ten minutes that otherwise evaporate at your desk. Lunch is a reliable trigger, so you never have to schedule it.

Sports
“Did yoga”

Ten minutes on the mat is enough. What counts isn’t the perfect sequence but body and mind winding down briefly every day.

Sports
“Did 5 pull-ups”

Few exercises show progress this honestly. What barely works on day 1 gets noticeably easier by day 30.

Sports
“Did 30 squats”

Works in the living room, at the office, even while brushing your teeth. And one day you notice the stairs have gotten easier.

Sports
“Danced to one song”

One song, loud, nobody watching. It counts as exercise but doesn't feel like it.

Sports
“Jumped rope for five minutes”

Five minutes of jumping rope is harder than it sounds. And a rope fits in any drawer.

Sports
“Brushed my teeth on one leg”

Two minutes of balance training that are already part of your day. After a few days, switch legs.

Health

Health challenges: take better care of yourself

Sleep, food, hydration: health challenges look unspectacular, but few categories change everyday life as noticeably. The strongest ones are addition challenges: adding something is far easier to sustain for a month than banning something.

15 ideas

Health
“Drank 2 liters of water”

Easy to measure with a bottle on your desk, and one of the few effects you notice as early as week one.

Health
“In bed before midnight”

Bedtime is a habit. 30 identical evenings shift your body clock more reliably than any alarm.

Health
“Ate no sugar”

30 days is the ideal window: long enough for cravings to fade, short enough to see it through.

Health
“Drank no alcohol”

The classic dry month. A fixed end date makes going without easier than a vague “drink less.”

Health
“Fruit or veg with every meal”

Forbids nothing, only adds. Challenges that add something are easier to sustain than bans.

Health
“Meditated for 10 minutes”

The benefit comes from regularity, not duration. That’s exactly what a daily circle captures.

Health
“Went to bed without my phone”

One clear evening rule that changes your entire sleep. And it’s binary, made for checking off.

Health
“Got 10 minutes of morning daylight”

The short walk before your first coffee sets your body clock. After a month you won’t want to give it up.

Health
“Flossed my teeth”

Ten seconds of effort, and most people still fail at it. That’s exactly why it works as a challenge: the resistance sits in the repetition, not the effort.

Health
“Took my vitamins”

The smallest health challenge there is: zero effort, and the daily ritual paves the way for bigger routines.

Health
“Didn't eat after 8 p.m.”

A fixed time is easier to stick to than any diet. And at 8:01 p.m. you know exactly whether the day counts.

Health
“Ate no meat”

A month without meat inevitably leads you to dishes you'd never have tried otherwise.

Health
“No coffee after 2 p.m.”

Afternoon coffee is often still working when you're already in bed. A 2 p.m. cutoff is easy to set.

Health
“Put on sunscreen”

Takes a minute and protects your skin even on grey days. At some point you reach for the tube without thinking.

Health
“Took a cold shower”

The last 30 seconds cold are enough to start. It'll wake you up, that's for sure.

Learning

Learning challenges: a little smarter every day

Learning rarely fails for lack of time; it fails for lack of regularity. A 30-day learning challenge flips that: a small daily dose, and the streak takes over the discipline.

15 ideas

Learning
“Studied a language for 15 minutes”

15 daily minutes beat any weekend marathon. Vocabulary sticks through spacing and repetition, not volume.

Learning
“Read 20 pages”

20 pages a day adds up to two or three books a month, but the challenge only counts today, never the pile.

Learning
“Worked 30 minutes on an online course”

Courses rarely fail on content; they fail on follow-through, and that’s exactly what the streak is for.

Learning
“Listened to one podcast episode on my field”

Fits into a commute or the dishes. The challenge costs no extra time, just one decision.

Learning
“Coded for 30 minutes”

Side projects die in the gap between two evenings of work. A daily minimum keeps the thread alive.

Learning
“Solved one chess puzzle”

Takes five minutes but has the character of a daily ritual: ideal for learning the habit of checking off itself.

Learning
“Summarized something I learned in three sentences”

Explaining exposes gaps. Three sentences a day turn passive consumption into active learning.

Learning
“Read one page”

Sounds absurdly small, and that’s the point. If 20 pages a day defeats you, one page still gets you into the book every evening.

Learning
“Looked up one thing I didn’t know”

What you don’t know during the day is forgotten minutes later. One answer a day adds up to a surprising amount over a month.

Learning
“Learned a new word”

One word a day sounds like nothing and adds up to 30 in a month. Few challenges are this easy to keep and this hard to forget.

Learning
“Practised touch typing”

A skill with a clear before and after: practise 30 days straight and you end up typing without looking, with the speed to prove it.

Learning
“Watched a documentary”

Swaps evening scrolling for something that sticks: 30 days, 30 topics, no study pressure.

Learning
“Practiced mental math”

A few sums in your head instead of on a calculator. Keeps you sharp, and it comes back faster than you'd think.

Learning
“Taught someone something”

Explaining is when you find out whether you really understood something. The other person learns along the way.

Learning
“Wrote down a mistake and its lesson”

One sentence on what you'll do differently next time. That turns a bad moment into something useful.

Creativity

Creative challenges: make something

Creative routines live on volume: what counts is not the one perfect piece but 30 imperfect attempts. That’s why creative challenges work best with mini-quotas, because the hurdle is never skill, it’s starting.

13 ideas

Creativity
“Wrote in my journal”

The value only emerges from the series. 30 scattered entries are nice, 30 in a row are a portrait of your month.

Creativity
“Drew one sketch”

Drawing is learned through volume, not talent. 30 bad sketches are the fastest route to better ones.

Creativity
“Practiced my instrument for 10 minutes”

Muscle memory builds overnight. Ten minutes a day beat the one hour on Sunday.

Creativity
“Composed one deliberate photo”

You carry the camera anyway; the challenge only trains your eye and leaves you a 30-picture diary as a bonus.

Creativity
“Wrote 200 words”

200 words is deliberately little. The hurdle is starting, and a small daily quota clears exactly that.

Creativity
“Wrote down three ideas”

Ideas come from practice, not inspiration. After 30 days you have 90 of them, and a mind that searches on its own.

Creativity
“Summed up the day in one sentence”

The miniature version of journaling, and the only one that survives a full day. One sentence still works at 11 p.m., a whole entry doesn’t.

Creativity
“Worked 10 minutes on my own project”

Side projects don’t die from lack of time, they die from long gaps. Ten minutes keeps the thread.

Creativity
“Wrote by hand instead of typing”

Handwriting forces you to slow down, and that changes what comes out. One sentence is enough for the check mark.

Creativity
“Composed music”

Creative work needs attempts, not strokes of genius. Tinker with ideas for 30 days and you end up with material instead of excuses.

Creativity
“Made something creative”

The most open creative challenge: paint, craft, cook by feel, as long as you make something instead of only consuming.

Creativity
“Knitted, crocheted or sewed”

A few rows in the evening and the piece grows. Handwork is calming, and in the end you're holding something real.

Creativity
“Folded an origami figure”

A sheet of paper, ten minutes, a set of instructions. By the end of the month there's a little paper zoo on the shelf.

Focus

Focus challenges: lock out distraction

Focus challenges are mostly subtraction challenges: no feed, no screen, no phone at the table. That’s exactly what a clear 30-day frame is for: you can negotiate a month with yourself, but not “forever.”

12 ideas

Focus
“Used no social media”

The feed is a habit loop, and 30 days is enough to break it. After that, you get to decide again.

Focus
“First hour of the day without my phone”

How the day starts is how it continues. This challenge moves the most important decision to the moment you wake up.

Focus
“Worked 25 minutes without interruption”

25 focused minutes a day sounds like nothing, but the series is what counts. Focus is a muscle, not a state.

Focus
“Put my phone away during meals”

Small, concrete, three chances a day, and still one of the most noticeable differences in everyday life.

Focus
“Wrote tomorrow’s to-do list”

Five minutes in the evening save the aimless first hour of the morning, and the effect compounds with every day of the streak.

Focus
“No screens after 9 p.m.”

A fixed screen curfew is easier to keep than “less phone”: clear boundaries beat vague intentions.

Focus
“Did a two-minute task right away”

Anything under two minutes otherwise lands on the list and stays there. One a day keeps the pile shrinking.

Focus
“Checked email only at set times”

It isn’t the volume that costs you focus, it’s the interruption. Two fixed windows a day are almost always enough.

Focus
“Started with the most important task”

Before email and chats take over your day, the most important thing is already done. Everything else looks smaller after that.

Focus
“Kept my phone in my pocket while waiting”

At the checkout, the bus stop, the waiting room, we reach for our phones almost automatically. Just waiting feels odd at first, but it's restful.

Focus
“Checked the news just once a day”

The state of the world doesn't change by the minute. Once a day is enough to stay informed.

Focus
“Finished work on time”

Work fills whatever time you give it. With a fixed end, the day before it often gets more productive.

Finance

Finance challenges: stay on top of your money

With money, the month works twice: it’s the natural budgeting unit, and at the end there’s a concrete amount as proof. Savings challenges don’t need big sums, just the daily repetition.

12 ideas

Finance
“Saved 5 € €”

The amount doesn’t matter, the habit does. After 30 days, not saving feels wrong, and 150 € are sitting there anyway.

Finance
“Logged every expense €”

Not saving, just looking: the act of logging alone changes what you buy, and the month hands you your true spending picture.

Finance
“Bought nothing unnecessary €”

A month without unnecessary spending. The clear end date turns restraint into a game instead of a punishment.

Finance
“Brought food instead of buying it €”

One single evening routine that pays off 30 times: at the end of the month the money is in your account, not in theory.

Finance
“Let impulse buys sit for 24 hours €”

The rule is tiny, the effect isn’t. Most buying impulses don’t survive a night.

Finance
“Checked my balance €”

Takes five seconds and still gets avoided for weeks. After 30 days, looking at your account costs no effort at all.

Finance
“Bought no takeaway coffee €”

Small enough that it doesn’t hurt, and by the end of the month there’s a two-figure sum on the right side of the ledger.

Finance
“Spent nothing all day €”

Rent and the weekly shop don’t count, this is about the spontaneous buys in between. They only become visible once you count them.

Finance
“Paid in cash, not by card €”

Cash makes spending tangible, because the wallet visibly empties. That’s precisely the effect contactless payment removes.

Finance
“Set money aside €”

Whether it's 1 € or 10 €: daily repetition turns setting money aside into a reflex, and the balance proves it at the end of the month.

Finance
“Used up leftovers instead of tossing them €”

Check what's still there first, then go shopping. Saves money and rescues half the fridge along the way.

Finance
“Spent ten minutes learning about money €”

Interest, ETFs, tax returns: topics people love to put off. In small portions they start to make sense, bit by bit.

Before you start

How to pick the right challenge

One, not five. The most common mistake isn’t the wrong challenge; it’s too many at once. Start with one: at twelve challenges a year, the next one is coming next month anyway.

Small enough for your worst day. A 30-day challenge doesn’t have to work on day 4 when you’re motivated, but on day 17 when you’re tired. When in doubt, take the smaller version: 10 minutes instead of 30, around the block instead of 5K.

Attach it to something that already exists. The challenges that stick dock onto a fixed point in your day: after brushing your teeth, on the way home, before the first coffee. Then you never have to decide again, just check it off.

Common questions

Questions about challenge ideas

What can you do for a challenge?

Anything you can repeat daily and answer with a clear yes or no in the evening. The most common areas are sports, health, learning, creativity, focus, finance and everyday life: 30 minutes of exercise, 2 liters of water, 20 pages read, 5 € saved, no social media. The 112 examples above cover all eight categories.

Which challenge is right for me?

The one you can still manage on your worst day, not the most ambitious one. A good monthly challenge is just done or not done, it’s possible on every single day, and it rarely takes more than 30 minutes. When in doubt, take the smaller version, and start with one instead of five.

How do you make a challenge?

You set a goal that can be answered yes or no in the evening, then run it for 30 days. That’s all it takes. The wording decides everything: “Read 20 pages” works, “read more” doesn’t, because you can’t check it off. After that, anything that makes progress visible helps, so you don’t renegotiate with yourself every day.

What makes a good daily challenge?

One that fits into a few minutes and still earns a real check mark every day: make the bed, tidy up for 10 minutes, take the stairs instead of the elevator, write down three things you’re grateful for. Daily challenges like these are the best way in, because they’re small enough that failing is hard. If a daily challenge carries you through 30 days, you pitched it small enough.

How long should a challenge last?

One month is the proven window: long enough for a habit to settle and old patterns to fade, short enough that the end stays in sight. You can negotiate a month with yourself; you can’t negotiate “forever”. That’s exactly what 12 Challenges is built around, with twelve challenges a year. Shorter formats like a 24-hour challenge or a single week are experiments rather than habits: they show you quickly whether something suits you, but they change little. Planning a longer stretch, say the three months of a Winter Arc? Then line up several months rather than stretching one.

Any tips for sticking with a challenge for 30 days?

Three things help most: set the bar low enough, attach the challenge to something that already exists in your day (after brushing your teeth, on the way home, before the first coffee), and keep your progress visible. A single missed day isn’t a failure, it’s normal. What matters is picking it back up the day after.
